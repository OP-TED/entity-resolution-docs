= ERSys Architecture
:description: Normative baseline architecture for the Entity Resolution System.

== 1. Introduction and reading guide

=== 1.1 Purpose of this document

This document defines the normative system architecture for the ERSys. It translates the agreed Product Requirements Document (PRD) into a coherent and implementable architectural baseline. +
The purpose of this document is to establish architectural authority. It specifies system boundaries, responsibilities, behavioural guarantees, and architectural invariants that must be respected by all components of ERSys and by any implementation claiming conformance to this architecture. +
This document is not a feature catalogue, a detailed design specification, or an operational manual. It deliberately avoids prescribing internal algorithms, technology stacks, deployment tooling, or user-interface implementations. Instead, it defines the minimum set of architectural commitments required to enable consistent and interoperable implementations, support safe integration with external systems, preserve semantic integrity over time, and allow controlled evolution without architectural drift. +
Where multiple design or implementation options exist, only those decisions that are architecturally significant for system coherence and integrity are captured here. Detailed artefacts such as API specifications, schemas, configuration files, deployment descriptors, and operational procedures are referenced where necessary but are maintained outside this document. +
In case of conflict, this architecture document takes precedence over earlier solution descriptions, prototype documentation, or informal design notes, unless explicitly superseded by a later version of this document or by a formally approved update to the PRD and corresponding ADR revision.

=== 1.2 Intended audience

This document is intended for the following audiences:

* Product owners and commissioning stakeholders, who require a stable and reviewable description of system scope, guarantees, and explicit non-goals.
* Enterprise, solution, and system architects, responsible for positioning ERSys within a broader architectural landscape and ensuring boundary alignment.
* Implementation and integration roles, who require clear responsibility separation, explicit contracts, and well-defined behavioural expectations.
* Reviewers and auditors, who need to understand what ERSys is accountable for, what it explicitly does not do, and how architectural consistency is enforced.

Readers are not expected to be familiar with internal resolution algorithms or specific technology choices. They are expected to be comfortable with architectural concepts such as system boundaries, contracts, authority, and invariants.

=== 1.3 How to read this document

This document is structured to progress from authority and intent, through behavioural guarantees, to structural realisation. +
Guidance for readers:

* Sections 2–4 establish architectural authority by defining system boundaries, roles, responsibility separation, architectural principles, and non-negotiable invariants.
* White-level Use Cases define the externally observable behaviour of ERSys. They specify what the system guarantees under defined conditions, without constraining internal algorithms or technologies.
* Behavioural spine flows identify the small set of interaction patterns that fundamentally characterise the system: resolution intake, asynchronous engine interaction, human curation, and rebuild operations. These flows are architectural commitments rather than illustrative examples.
* Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) capture binding architectural decisions and invariants that constrain system design and evolution. ADRs provide both normative constraints and explanatory context. Where this document consolidates or restates ADR decisions, it shall do so consistently with the authoritative ADR text.
* Annexes contain supporting material such as Blue-level Use Cases, the Business Glossary, ADR texts, and contract references. These annexes support consistent interpretation but do not replace the normative statements in the main body.
Readers seeking a rapid understanding of ERSys authority and guarantees should focus on Sections 2, 4, 7, and 8. Readers involved in implementation, integration, or review should treat the document as a whole.

=== 1.4 Terminology and interpretation

The Business Glossary (Annex A) defines the authoritative meaning of domain and system terms used throughout this document. +
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all occurrences of glossary terms, whether capitalised or not, are interpreted according to their glossary definitions and are used consistently across architectural views, use cases, decision records, and interface descriptions. +
Where a term is used in a specialised or contextual sense that deviates from its glossary definition, that deviation is stated explicitly and applies only within the stated scope. +
This rule is normative. It ensures consistent interpretation across the architecture, prevents semantic drift as the system evolves, and supports traceability between requirements, architectural decisions, and implementation artefacts.

=== 1.5 Diagram notation and interpretation

This document uses a small, controlled set of modelling notations to express architectural structure, behaviour, and information concepts. +
ArchiMate notation is used as the primary enterprise architecture language, particularly for Business and Application layer views that describe system boundaries, actors, roles, application components, and their relationships. These diagrams are normative with respect to scope, responsibility, and interaction boundaries. +
UML sequence diagrams are used selectively to illustrate behavioural spine flows. They express interaction order and responsibility transfer but do not introduce additional structural elements beyond those defined in ArchiMate views. +
UML class diagrams are used for conceptual information modelling, including domain concepts and data structures relevant to entity resolution. These models describe conceptual structure and relationships and do not prescribe physical schemas, storage technologies, or persistence strategies. +
In some cases, diagrams adopt composition and containment conventions inspired by the C4 model to improve readability. Where this occurs, C4-style composition is applied using ArchiMate elements and semantics and does not introduce additional modelling concepts or abstraction levels. +
All diagrams are to be interpreted according to the semantics of their underlying notation. Visual layout, grouping, or proximity alone must not be interpreted as implying behaviour, ownership, or dependency unless explicitly represented by a defined relationship.

=== 1.6 Conceptual framing and identity model

The architecture of ERSys starts from a simple but strict distinction that we must take seriously if we want the system to remain coherent over time. We distinguish between real-world entities, information artefacts, and managed identity constructs inside the system. In many data integration projects these layers are mixed, often implicitly, and this is where confusion begins. Here we make the separation explicit and structural, because every interface, persistence decision, and authority boundary depends on it. +
Real-world entities are the referents that exist independently of ERSys. They are organisations, persons, procedures, or other domain objects that exist whether or not the system observes them. ERSys does not store them, version them, or model them in an ontological sense. It processes descriptions provided by external actors. One may think of it in semantic web terms: the system never touches the thing-in-the-world, it only processes statements about that thing. This may sound abstract, but it prevents a common mistake, which is to assume that the system somehow owns the truth about the entity. +
Those statements enter ERSys as entity mentions. An entity mention is an immutable information artefact submitted by an originator within a specific operational context. It carries descriptive content expressed in a concrete representation such as RDF, together with correlation metadata. Each mention is identified by the tuple (sourceId, requestId, entityType). This identifier supports traceability and idempotency. It does not express canonical identity. A mention may be duplicated, incomplete, inconsistent, or wrong. That is not an error condition in itself. Mentions describe. They do not decide equivalence. +
Identity in ERSys emerges through clustering in the Entity Resolution Engine. A cluster is the system’s identity construct. The identifier assigned to a cluster, the clusterId, is the canonical identifier exposed to clients. Canonical identity is therefore equivalent to cluster identity in the ERE. There is no independent registry inside ERS that grants identity status apart from clustering outcomes. The ERE cluster store is the single source of truth for identity semantics. ERS projects and exposes the latest assignment returned by the engine. +
Cluster identifiers are stable in the sense that they are never reused for unrelated clusters. Once a clusterId has been created, it remains reserved. Stability, however, concerns the existence and non-reuse of the identifier, not the immutability of assignment. A mention may move from one cluster to another as resolution improves, models evolve, or new evidence appears. Stability protects references used by downstream systems. It does not freeze classification. This distinction is essential if we want long-lived identifiers and evolving resolution quality to coexist. +
Resolution itself is asynchronous inside the engine, while clients expect bounded synchronous responses from ERS. These two temporal models must be reconciled carefully. When the ERE does not respond within the configured execution window, ERS may issue a deterministically derived singleton clusterId. This provisional identifier is a valid canonical identifier at the moment of response. It anchors identity immediately so that downstream processing can continue. Later, when the ERE completes evaluation, the assignment may be confirmed or replaced. The client-facing contract remains simple, while engine authority over clustering is preserved. +
It is also important to separate identity from description. A cluster is an identity construct. It does not assert factual truth about a real-world entity. Any descriptive artefacts associated with a cluster are treated as data and may evolve over time. They can be curated, regenerated, or removed without affecting the canonical identifier itself. In knowledge graph vocabulary, one may say that the clusterId functions as a stable reference, comparable to an IRI, while the attached representations remain contingent assertions. +
ERSys follows an engine-authoritative model. The ERE determines clustering outcomes. ERS orchestrates interaction, enforces time budgets, maintains the projection of the latest cluster assignment per mention, and exposes canonical identifiers through stable APIs. User actions and provisional placements are forwarded to the ERE as recommendations. They contribute evidence and influence computation, yet the final clustering outcome remains under engine authority. This keeps the semantics of identity consistent and avoids hidden governance layers emerging at the façade level. +
Taken together, this framing positions ERSys as an identity-projection system built on clear responsibility boundaries. It separates descriptive content from identity constructs, separates computation from authority, and separates synchronous exposure from asynchronous correction. These distinctions may appear subtle when read in isolation, yet they prevent semantic drift and architectural ambiguity as the system evolves. The precise definitions of the concepts used here are provided in the Business Glossary and are applied consistently throughout the architecture.
