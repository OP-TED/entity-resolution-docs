== 5. Business context

This section explains the external objectives, strategic assumptions, and non-functional expectations that shaped the architectural posture of ERSys. It is explanatory and informative. It does not introduce new guarantees and shall not override the normative rules defined in earlier sections. +
ERSys exists to support referential interoperability across autonomous systems that do not share data ownership, internal models, or quality processes. The architectural consequence is deliberate restraint. ERSys anchors identity through canonical identifiers derived from clustering outcomes, but it does not consolidate, enrich, or harmonise domain data. Identity alignment is the concern; data centralisation is not. +
A central architectural driver was the requirement to enable independently developed and replaceable implementations of the Entity Resolution Engine. This requirement led to the formalisation of the ERS–ERE Technical Contract as a normative boundary artefact. The contract defines message semantics, correlation identifiers, idempotency expectations, and canonical identifier derivation rules. By stabilising this contract, ERSys allows independently developed ERE implementations to interoperate predictably with ERS without embedding engine-specific logic into the core system, provided they respect the canonical identifier value space and clustering authority model defined in this baseline. Architectural clarity and long-term evolvability depend on this separation. +
Operational expectations further shaped the design. Client-facing interactions are required to complete within configured time budgets. The system therefore prioritises bounded responsiveness over synchronous completeness. When clustering cannot conclude within the execution window, ERS preserves responsiveness by issuing a provisional singleton canonical identifier and integrates subsequent authoritative ERE outcomes asynchronously, replacing provisional assignments where necessary. This reflects a pragmatic balance between user experience and computational variability. +
The business context also assumes that entity evidence accumulates and clustering quality evolves. Identity assignments may therefore change over time. Stability is achieved through deterministic identifier value generation and explicit refresh mechanisms rather than through immutable assignment. Downstream systems are expected to resynchronise based on exposed state changes. +
Scalability and partial failure are treated as normal operating conditions. Asynchronous cooperation between ERS and ERE, idempotent message handling, and strict contract boundaries prevent tight coupling and reduce systemic fragility. Authority is separated: clustering logic resides in the ERE, while ERS governs exposure, time budgets, and persistence of the latest recorded outcomes. +
Finally, the scope defined in the offer excludes master data management, external registry reconciliation, model retraining, and compliance-grade audit capabilities. The architecture reflects this by maintaining only the latest recorded clustering decision per mention and a read-only log of user recommendations, without constructing lineage graphs, canonical history models, or consolidated entity records. +
Taken together, these drivers explain the architectural stance of ERSys: a contract-centred identity anchoring system that enables independent engine implementations, bounded interaction, controlled evolution of assignments, and disciplined separation of responsibility.
